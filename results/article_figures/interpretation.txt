INTERPRETATION OF THE ARTICLE FIGURES
=====================================

Scope and reading precautions
-----------------------------
The figures compare two formulations of the same benchmark instances: a
changeover-aware model, which includes product-change costs during optimization,
and a model that ignores those costs. For a fair economic comparison, the
solution obtained by the latter is evaluated ex post with its actual changeover
cost added. Throughout the figures, operational cost therefore means travelled
distance plus changeover cost.

The paired analyses contain 79 of the 100 benchmark instances. The other 21 are
excluded because at least one formulation did not return a usable solution.
Moreover, only 5 paired instances were proven optimal under both formulations;
most plotted solutions are incumbents obtained at the time limit. Consequently,
the figures provide strong descriptive evidence about the solutions found under
the computational budget, but they do not generally prove that one formulation's
true optimum is better than the other's. The exclusion of unsolved instances may
also make the paired results more representative of the tractable subset than of
the complete benchmark.


Figure 1 - Paired comparison of operational cost
------------------------------------------------
Each point represents one instance. Its horizontal coordinate is the total cost
of the solution found while ignoring changeover costs, after adding those costs
ex post; its vertical coordinate is the total cost of the changeover-aware
solution. Both axes are logarithmic, so positions reflect relative rather than
absolute differences over the wide range of instance costs. A point below the
dashed equal-cost line favours the changeover-aware solution; a point above the
line favours the solution obtained without explicit changeover costs.

Most points are below the diagonal: the changeover-aware incumbent is cheaper in
56 of 79 pairs, equal in 3, and more expensive in 20. The median net saving is
28.22%. Thus, explicitly accounting for changeovers usually produces a materially
lower evaluated operational cost, although the result is not universal and a few
instances show large losses. The points near the upper-right diagonal indicate
that, for some expensive instances, both approaches find solutions with similar
total costs. The colour pattern also suggests that the advantage is more common
and larger for instances with two to four products, whereas five- and six-product
instances are more frequently close to, or above, the equal-cost line.

For the five pairs in which both runs were proven optimal, all five favour the
changeover-aware formulation; their median saving is 37.67%. This is a small
subset, so it supports the economic mechanism without being sufficient for a
broad optimality claim.


Figure 2 - Distance-changeover trade-off
----------------------------------------
The horizontal axis measures the extra distance travelled by the changeover-aware
solution relative to the solution found while ignoring changeovers. Positive
values mean extra distance; negative values mean that the aware solution is also
shorter. The vertical axis measures the changeover-cost saving achieved by the
aware solution. Positive values mean lower changeover cost. Since net saving is
changeover-cost saving minus additional distance, the dashed y = x line is the
economic break-even boundary: points above it favour the changeover-aware
solution, and points below it do not.

The points form an almost vertical cloud around zero additional distance, while
changeover-cost differences span a much larger range. The median distance
increase is only 61.77 cost units, compared with a median changeover-cost saving
of 11,583 units. In 49 pairs the aware solution travels farther, in 25 it travels
less far, and in 5 the reported distance is unchanged. Nevertheless, 56 pairs lie
on the beneficial side of break-even. This indicates that the principal economic
effect comes from reorganizing routes to avoid expensive product transitions,
usually at a comparatively small distance penalty. Some points have negative
changeover savings: for those time-limited runs, the aware incumbent actually has
higher reported changeover cost, showing that explicit modelling does not ensure
a better incumbent within the available solve time.


Figure 3 - Effect by number of products
---------------------------------------
The left panel groups percentage net cost savings by product count. Values above
zero favour explicit changeover modelling; values below zero favour the ignored-
cost solution. The right panel similarly reports the reduction in the number of
product changes. The boxes summarize each group, while the overlaid dots retain
the individual observations and reveal the substantial within-group variation.

For two, three, and four products, median cost savings are respectively 42.48%,
41.67%, and 42.98%. Their median reductions in product changes are 2.5, 3, and 6.
The median benefit drops sharply at five products: the median saving is only
1.72% and the median reduction in changes is zero. With six products, the median
saving is -1.24%, while the median reduction is one change. Thus the results do
not show a monotonic increase in benefit as the number of products grows. Instead,
the strongest observed economic and operational effects occur for two to four
products, followed by a marked weakening for five and six products.

This pattern should be interpreted cautiously because group sizes are unequal
(20, 25, 11, 17, and 6 instances for two through six products), especially for
six products, and other instance characteristics are not controlled. The figure
therefore shows an association with product count, not a causal product-count
effect. The wide boxes and extreme dots also show strong instance dependence:
even in groups with a positive median, some aware incumbents are worse, and a few
instances obtain very large benefits.


Figure 4 - Solver outcomes under the time limit
-----------------------------------------------
The stacked bars summarize all 100 benchmark instances. The changeover-aware
model proves 7 instances optimal, returns a feasible time-limit incumbent for 75,
and leaves 18 unsolved. The formulation that ignores changeover costs proves 6
optimal, returns 76 time-limit incumbents, and also leaves 18 unsolved.

The nearly identical bars show no meaningful aggregate difference in the ability
of the two formulations to produce a usable solution under the time limit. They
also show that exact certification is rare: more than three quarters of all
instances terminate with an incumbent rather than a proof of optimality. This is
why differences in Figures 1-3 should primarily be described as differences in
incumbent quality under a fixed computational budget.


Figure 5 - Computational effort and instance size
-------------------------------------------------
Runtime is plotted against the number of stations on a logarithmic vertical
scale. Circles denote proven-optimal runs and crosses denote runs stopped at the
time limit. The left and right panels correspond to the aware and ignored-cost
formulations. Only the 79 complete paired instances appear here, so unsolved runs
are absent.

In both panels, almost all observations are crosses concentrated at the common
time-limit ceiling. Proven-optimal solutions occur mainly among small instances,
with only a few larger exceptions. This is evidence that the benchmark is
computationally demanding and that exact solution becomes uncommon as station
count grows. However, the large amount of right-censoring prevents a meaningful
runtime-growth curve from being read from the plot: a point at the ceiling says
only that the run used the allotted time, not how much longer it would have needed
to prove optimality. The two panels again look very similar, so there is no clear
evidence here that explicitly including changeover costs materially changes
runtime or scalability relative to the ignored-cost formulation.


Overall conclusion
------------------
Under the tested time limit, explicit modelling of changeover costs usually
improves the evaluated operational cost of the returned solution. The improvement
is driven chiefly by savings in product-change costs and reductions in product
changes, with comparatively small changes in route distance. The observed benefit
is strongest for instances with two to four products and much weaker for five or
six. Computationally, both formulations behave similarly: they solve roughly the
same number of instances, but most runs reach the time limit without proving
optimality. The appropriate conclusion is therefore that changeover awareness
improves incumbent quality on most solved benchmark pairs, not that it has been
shown to improve the optimum on the full benchmark set.
